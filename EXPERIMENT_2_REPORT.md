# Experiment 2 — Negation detection: is student sentence B the negation of teacher sentence A?

Report written 2026-09-22. All code under `experiments/negation_detection/`; all
numbers in this document come from `experiments/negation_detection/results.json`,
`experiments/negation_detection/results_mpnet.json` and the dataset files under
`experiments/negation_detection/data/`. Tables were generated by
`experiments/negation_detection/report_experiment2.py`, not typed; each table
block says which file it came from.

## 0. Status summary

The full pipeline ran end to end on CPU: the four datasets were downloaded and
built, the stage-1 generator was run over all 3,924 distinct A sentences, and all
three architectures plus three baselines were evaluated with 5-fold
cross-validation on phase 1 (3,002 CANNOT pairs), once on the held-out mixed
phase 2 (2,001 pairs), and once on xNot360 (360 pairs). **The headline result is
negative for the architectures this project cares about: an off-the-shelf NLI
cross-encoder (`cross-encoder/nli-deberta-v3-base`) beats every embedding- or
generator-based scorer by a wide margin — phase-1 CV ROC-AUC 0.993 vs 0.744 for
the best of ours, and a paraphrase false-positive rate of 3.0% vs 36–93% — and
feeding the generator's reference sets into that cross-encoder makes it worse,
not better.** Both embedding models were run
(`all-MiniLM-L6-v2` and `all-mpnet-base-v2`), and **the choice of embedding
model changes which of our architectures looks best**: with MiniLM the hybrid
leads on phase 2 (AUC 0.777 vs 0.686 for the reference-set scorer), with mpnet
it is the reverse and by more (0.507 vs 0.752). One component is partial:
architecture 2 under the NLI similarity function was run on a 300-pair subsample
per split rather than in full (the full version is ~110k cross-encoder passes),
and only for MiniLM. The single biggest caveat is the data: 61% of CANNOT's
"negations" contain no negation cue at all and many are arbitrary factual
substitutions rather than negations (verbatim examples in §2), so both the
absolute numbers and the ranking may not transfer to real student writing.

### 2.2 Filtering and label assignment

**CANNOT.** Labels are taken as released: `label=1` means the hypothesis is a
negation of the premise, `label=0` means it is a paraphrase. Nothing was
filtered out on content; sampling is by quota (§2.3). The dataset has no
provenance column, so the source corpus of each pair was **recovered** by
normalised exact-match of either sentence against the per-source processed files
shipped in the same repository (`data.py::source_map`). 77,365 of 77,376 pairs
matched; 11 are tagged `unattributed`.

**Negation type (verbal / antonym / other) is derived, not given**
(`data.py::negation_type`), in this order:

1. source is `antonym-substitution` (SemAntoNeg) → `antonym`, since that corpus
   is antonym substitution by construction;
2. otherwise `cue_count(B) != cue_count(A)` → `verbal`, where `cue_count` is the
   project's own lexicon counter (`experiments/negation_direction/experiment.py`);
3. otherwise a replaced word is a WordNet antonym of the word it replaced
   (`data.py::has_antonym_swap`, using `difflib.SequenceMatcher` over word lists
   and `negation.nlp_core.antonyms`) → `antonym`;
4. otherwise → `other`.

**STS-Benchmark**: test split only, kept where `score < 2.0` (441 of 1,379
qualify, 167 sampled), labelled 0, subtype `sts_low`.
**SNLI**: validation split, `label == 1` (neutral) only (3,235 available, 167
sampled), labelled 0, subtype `snli_neutral`.
**Random re-pairings**: sentence 1 of one STS-B/SNLI pair joined to sentence 2 of
a different one after independent shuffles, labelled 0, subtype `random_pair`.
All three are additionally required to use sentences not already claimed by any
other pair.
**xNot360**: taken whole; its `label` (1 = the two sentences logically negate
each other) is used as-is.

### 2.3 Sampling and disjointness

Quotas are allocated proportionally to each source's share of the label within
CANNOT (`data.py::quotas`), with a floor of one per source. Pairs are grouped by
normalised A (`data.py::assign_phases`); a group is assigned to one phase whole;
a pair is skipped if either of its sentences was already claimed by the other
phase. Normalisation is `re.sub(r"\s+", " ", text.strip().lower()).rstrip(" .")`.

### 2.5 Known data quality problems

Measured (table above) and inspected:

1. **Pairs where A is textually identical to B.** 10 in phase 1, 6 in phase 2.
   One of them is labelled `1` (negation) in phase 1 — an outright mislabel in
   CANNOT:
   `A = "In 2009, UNESCO was researching a possible British reintroduction of the Mazarine blue to Britain."`,
   `B` identical, `label = 1`, source `wikifactcheck-english`. No scorer can get
   that pair right.
2. **Many CANNOT "negations" are not negations.** Sampling label-1 pairs
   (§2.4 table) turns up substitutions that change a fact without negating
   anything: *"His father was the nephew and heir of Dr Alexander Henry
   Haliday."* → *"His father was the **grandson** and heir…"*; *"It was first
   developed as a telephony technology."* → *"…as a **telescoping** technology
   only."*; *"Such churches are referred to as cathedrals."* → *"…as
   **immemorial basilicas**."* These are the 713 phase-1 positives in the
   `other` bucket (47% of positives). This is label noise with respect to the
   task as this project defines it, and it is the reason the lexical baseline
   collapses (§4).
3. **Double negation is labelled as paraphrase, correctly but confusingly.**
   *"It's personal, guys."* → *"It's **not impersonal**, guys."* is `label=0`
   (SemAntoNeg). Semantically right — it is the K1 cancellation case the
   generator models — but it means phase 1 contains paraphrase pairs whose B has
   *more* negation cues than A, which is exactly the pattern a cue-counting
   baseline calls a negation.
4. **A is already negated in a large minority of pairs**: 834 of 3,002 phase-1
   pairs and 453 of 2,001 phase-2 pairs have at least one lexicon cue in A. The
   "negation direction" framing assumes A is the affirmative; it often is not.
5. **Short sentences**: 231 phase-1 and 152 phase-2 pairs have a sentence under
   20 characters (mostly `sentiment-labelled-sentences`, e.g. *"will definitely
   return!"*). These carry little signal for any embedding method.
6. **No duplicate (A, B) pairs** within either phase, and only 6 (phase 1) / 1
   (phase 2) repeated B strings across different A.

## 3. Method — as implemented

Component paths are `experiments/negation_detection/` unless stated. Reused
helpers come from `experiments/negation_direction/experiment.py`.

### 3.1 Architecture 1 — feature scorer (`detect.py::pair_features`, `run_split`)

The negation axis is fit by `detect.py::fit_axis` on **training rows only**:

```
axis = unit( mean over train pairs with label==1 of ( emb(B) - emb(A) ) )
```

`unit` is L2 normalisation (imported from the negation_direction experiment).
In cross-validation the training rows are the four training folds; for the
phase-2 and xNot360 evaluations they are all 3,002 phase-1 pairs. Embeddings are
unit-norm, so cosine is a dot product. The six features, in the order the
coefficients are reported:

| feature | formula |
|---|---|
| `p_a` | `emb(A) · axis` |
| `p_b` | `emb(B) · axis` |
| `delta_p` | `p_b - p_a` |
| `c_raw` | `emb(A) · emb(B)` |
| `c_resid` | `resid(A) · resid(B)` where `resid(x) = normalise(x - (x·axis) axis)` (`detect.py::residual`) |
| `cue_delta` | `cue_count(B) - cue_count(A)`, the lexicon counter from `experiments/negation_direction/experiment.py::cue_count` (the project's own word lists plus `-less` / `un-` / `non-` / `im-` / `ir-` / `il-` affix rules) |

Classifier: `sklearn.linear_model.LogisticRegression(max_iter=5000)` — default
L2 penalty, `C=1.0`, `lbfgs`. Features are standardised with the **training**
mean and standard deviation before fitting, so the reported coefficients are
comparable across features. Score = `predict_proba(...)[:, 1]`.

### 3.2 Architecture 2 — reference-set comparison (`refsets.py`, `detect.py::arch2_scores`)

`refsets.py` runs the unmodified stage-1 generator (`negation.generate_all`) on
each of the 3,924 distinct A sentences in batches of 200, writing
`data/refsets.jsonl`. Each generated variant is sorted by **net polarity
relative to A**:

```
flipped = (variant.net_negation % 2) != (A.input_cue_count % 2)
NEG  = variants where flipped
SAME = variants where not flipped, plus A itself
```

So a K1 cancellation (`net_negation = 0`) and a K2 compound
(`net_negation = 2`) both land in SAME when A is affirmative, and a variant with
one cue lands in NEG; when A is itself negated the rule inverts, which is the
point of using parity rather than a cue count. `test_negation_detection.py::
test_reference_sets_split_on_net_polarity` asserts this over 500 records.

For each pair, with `b = emb(B)`:

| similarity | definition (maximised over each set) |
|---|---|
| (i) raw cosine | `cos(B, v)` |
| (ii) residual + polarity | `resid(v) · resid(B) − \|p(v) − p(B)\|` — content agreement off the axis, penalised by distance along it |
| (iii) NLI | `P(entailment)` of the pair `(v, B)` from the cross-encoder |

`s_neg = max over NEG`, `s_same = max over SAME`, `margin = s_neg − s_same`. An
empty NEG set scores `s_neg = −1` (cosine) or `−2` (residual), i.e. the pair is
pushed towards "not a negation"; empty sets are counted as coverage failures and
reported separately. Similarity (iii) is `detect.py::arch2_nli`, run on 300
pairs per split with each reference set capped at its 8 members closest to B by
cosine (`NLI_REF_CAP = 8`, `NLI_SUBSAMPLE = 300`).

**Three-way decision** (`detect.py::three_way`): `unrelated` if
`max(s_neg, s_same)` is below a threshold, else `negation` if the margin clears
the margin threshold, else `agrees`. The margin threshold is the
F1-maximising one from the training rows. The unrelated threshold **cannot** be
fit against unrelated examples because phase 1 has none, so it is set to the 5th
percentile of the training pairs' `max(s_neg, s_same)` — "below what related
pairs look like". This is a deviation from the brief's wording (§1 row 9, §8).

### 3.3 Architecture 3 — hybrid (`detect.py::run_split`)

The six features of architecture 1 plus `margin_cos` and `margin_res`, same
standardisation, same logistic regression.

### 3.4 Baselines

| baseline | definition |
|---|---|
| raw cosine | score = `−c_raw`; the orientation assumes negations are *less* similar, which §4 shows is wrong on this data |
| lexical cue delta | score = `cue_delta` |
| NLI cross-encoder | `cross-encoder/nli-deberta-v3-base`, softmax over its three logits, score = `P(contradiction)` of the ordered pair `(A, B)`. No calibration, no fine-tuning, no threshold tuning beyond the same F1-maximising rule applied to the training rows. `detect.py::NLI`, cached on disk by `(premise, hypothesis)` in `cache/nli.pkl` |

### 3.5 Embeddings

`experiments/negation_direction/experiment.py::embed`: `AutoTokenizer` +
`AutoModel`, `max_length=256`, batch 32, mean pooling over the last hidden state
weighted by the attention mask, then L2 normalisation — the same pipeline
sentence-transformers uses for both models, computed with `transformers`
directly. Two caches: pair sentences share
`experiments/negation_direction/cache/{model}.pkl` with the first experiment;
the ~70k generated reference sentences go to
`experiments/negation_detection/cache/variants_{model}.pkl` (116 MB for MiniLM)
so they do not swamp the shared one. `test_cached_embeddings_are_l2_normalised`
checks the norms.

### 3.6 Splits, folds, thresholds

`detect.py::group_folds` shuffles the sorted distinct A sentences with
`numpy.random.default_rng(0)` and assigns them round-robin to 5 folds, so no A
appears in two folds (asserted by `test_cv_folds_are_grouped_by_sentence_a`).
Seed is 0 throughout. Thresholds come from `detect.py::best_threshold`, which
maximises F1 over up to 400 quantile-spaced candidates **on the rows it is
given** — training folds in CV, all of phase 1 for the phase-2 and xNot360
evaluations. Test rows are never used to fit an axis, a scaler, a classifier or
a threshold; the only thing computed on test rows is the score.

### 5.4 Reading of the error tables

*Measured* above; *inferred* here.

**Architecture 2 fails on exactly the construction the generator is good at.**
Its worst false positives are SemAntoNeg paraphrases of the form
*"It's a good idea."* → *"It's not an evil idea."*, *"It's natural."* →
*"It's not artificial."*, *"I'm innocent, sir."* → *"I'm not guilty, sir."*.
These are cancellations: *not* + antonym = the original meaning. The generator's
NEG set for A contains *"It's not a good idea."*, which is lexically one word
away from the student's *"It's not an evil idea."*, so `s_neg` wins and the
margin goes positive. The method matches on surface form, and surface form is
precisely what cancellation manipulates. The effect is measurable by source:
architecture 2's paraphrase false-positive rate is 0.634 on the 41 SemAntoNeg
paraphrases against 0.342 on the 398 WikiFactCheck ones. (SemAntoNeg supplies
only 26 of its 582 false positives overall — it is a small slice of phase 2 —
but it is where the method breaks most reliably.) This is the same failure the first
experiment found for the negation axis on K1 pairs, arriving by a different
route.

**Architecture 2's worst false negatives are coverage failures, mechanically.**
Every pair with `margin_cos = −2.0` is a pair where the generator produced no
NEG variant at all: `s_neg` is then the sentinel `−1` and `s_same ≈ +1` because B
is nearly identical to A. The examples are long, multi-clause review sentences
(*"Totally different, with loads of understatement and black comedy, this
isn't a film few get to see…"*) — true verbal negations with cosine 0.99 that
the architecture cannot see because it had nothing to compare them against.
Quantified: 80 of 2,001 phase-2 pairs (4.0%) have an empty NEG set, 37 of those
are positives, and architecture 2 recovers **0 of those 37**. Coverage failures
are 15.8% of its 234 false negatives while being 4% of the data — a four-fold
concentration.

**The hybrid's false positives are unrelated pairs, not paraphrases.** Its
fifteen highest-scoring errors are all `random_pair`, `sts_low` or
`snli_neutral` — B has nothing to do with A, cosine is near zero or negative,
and the logistic regression reads low similarity as evidence of negation
(coefficient on `c_raw` is strongly negative, §4). That is the predictable
consequence of training on phase 1, where every negative is a paraphrase: the
model never saw "unrelated" and has no way to represent it.

**The hybrid's false negatives are double negations.** Fourteen of its fifteen
lowest-scoring misses are antonym-type pairs where *both* sentences carry a cue —
*"Oh, it's not foul."* → *"Oh, it's not fair."*, *"This is not good."* →
*"This is not evil."*. `cue_delta` is 0, `delta_p` is near 0, cosine is 0.3–0.7,
so every feature says "same polarity" while the gold label says the pair flips.
These are genuinely hard: the negation lives in the antonym, under a cue that
does not move. Across all 140 of the hybrid's false negatives, 36 (25.7%) have
a cue in both sentences.

**Length effects are small for our scorers and real for the cross-encoder.**
The hybrid's accuracy is flat across length buckets; the NLI baseline improves
from 0.766 on pairs under 20 words to 0.863 on 20–40 words, and its
false-positive rate drops from 0.388 to 0.271. Short pairs are where the
cross-encoder's errors concentrate — and short pairs are exactly what
`sentiment-labelled-sentences` and SemAntoNeg contribute.

**Where the two disagree, the hybrid is not simply worse.** Of the 410 pairs
where the NLI baseline says "negation" and the hybrid says "not", the hybrid is
right on 272 — these are mostly unrelated pairs that the cross-encoder calls
contradictions (*"A man is playing a flute."* / *"A man is riding a scooter."*,
P(contradiction) 0.9999). Of the 410 in the other direction, the NLI baseline is
right on 386 — those are clean paraphrases the hybrid calls negations. The
cross-encoder's weakness is that *contradiction* and *negation* are not the same
relation; ours is that similarity is not polarity.

## 6. Comparison and interpretation

### 6.1 Which scorer wins, and is the gap real?

**Which of our architectures wins depends on the embedding model**, which is
itself a result:

| ROC-AUC | phase-1 CV | phase 2 | xNot360 |
|---|---|---|---|
| arch 1, MiniLM | 0.550 | 0.734 | 0.364 |
| arch 1, mpnet | 0.652 | 0.407 | 0.541 |
| arch 2 cosine, MiniLM | 0.744 | 0.686 | 0.642 |
| arch 2 cosine, mpnet | **0.805** | **0.752** | **0.676** |
| arch 3, MiniLM | 0.645 | 0.777 | 0.371 |
| arch 3, mpnet | 0.721 | 0.507 | 0.484 |
| NLI baseline (model-independent) | 0.993 | 0.834 | 0.940 |

With MiniLM the hybrid looks best on phase 2 (0.777); with mpnet it is the worst
of the three there (0.507) and the reference-set scorer is best (0.752). The
only scorer of ours that is stable across both models and all three splits is
architecture 2, which is also the only one that beats chance everywhere. The
feature scorer and the hybrid — the two that lean on the negation axis — are the
unstable ones.

Fold-to-fold standard deviations on phase 1 are small: 0.0013 (arch 2, MiniLM),
0.0022 (arch 2, mpnet), 0.0348 (arch 3, MiniLM), 0.0028 (NLI). The phase-1 gaps
are therefore many standard deviations wide and not fold noise, and so is the
model-to-model difference for architecture 2 (0.744 vs 0.805 against ~0.002 fold
noise). The gap between the NLI baseline and the best of ours (0.993 vs 0.805)
is roughly 70 fold-standard-deviations.

### 6.2 Does anything beat the NLI cross-encoder?

Not overall, and not on any subset large enough to matter. The two places where
something of ours is ahead:

- **Unrelated pairs.** The NLI baseline's false-positive rate is 0.922 on random
  re-pairings and 0.814 on STS-low, against 0.066 and 0.192 for the MiniLM
  hybrid. (Architecture 2 is no better than the cross-encoder here: 0.8855 and
  0.695 with MiniLM, 0.819 and 0.563 with mpnet.) The
  cross-encoder is detecting *contradiction*, which unrelated sentence pairs
  frequently exhibit, so this is a task-definition mismatch rather than a
  quality gap — but for a grading system that has to distinguish "wrong answer"
  from "opposite answer", it matters, and it is the one concrete argument for a
  composite system.
- **The disagreement region**, as above: 272 of 410 pairs where the hybrid
  overrules the cross-encoder, the hybrid is right — again concentrated on
  unrelated pairs.

On the pairs the task is actually about — negation vs paraphrase, phase 1 — the
cross-encoder wins everywhere: 0.993 against the best of ours at 0.805, and a
3.0% paraphrase false-positive rate on phase 2 against 18–93%.

### 6.3 Did phase-1 performance transfer?

See the table in §6.1. With MiniLM, architectures 1 and 3 *rise* from phase 1 to
phase 2 and then collapse on xNot360; with mpnet they fall at every step. The rise is not a generalisation success: phase 2 adds 500 unrelated
pairs, which are separable by plain similarity, and `c_raw` alone scores 0.738
AUC on phase 2 against 0.523 on phase 1. The collapse on xNot360 is the
informative half: xNot360's negatives are logical near-misses that share almost
all their words with the positives (mean `c_raw` 0.927 for negatives vs 0.912
for positives), so every similarity-derived feature points the wrong way and the
learned coefficients invert. Architecture 2 degrades least (0.744 → 0.686 →
0.642 with MiniLM, 0.805 → 0.752 → 0.676 with mpnet) and reaches 0.700 accuracy
on xNot360 with mpnet, the best of any non-NLI scorer there, because it does not
ask "how similar are A and B?" but "does B match a negation of A?", and that
question survives the distribution shift better. The NLI baseline degrades gracefully (0.993 → 0.834
→ 0.940 AUC) but its *accuracy* on xNot360 is only 0.744 because the threshold
carried over from CANNOT is badly calibrated there.

### 6.4 How much of this is CANNOT's rule-based construction?

CANNOT's two largest components were built by automatic negation
(WikiFactCheck-English rewrites) and automatic antonym substitution
(SemAntoNeg). Evidence bearing on how much the results ride on those artefacts:

- **Against a surface-pattern explanation for the NLI baseline:** the lexical
  cue-delta baseline — the scorer that would benefit most from a rule-based
  generator's fingerprints — scores 0.492 CV AUC, i.e. nothing. If the negations
  carried a consistent surface signature, cue counting would find it. It does
  not, because only 39% of the positives involve a cue at all.
- **For a shared-distribution explanation for the NLI baseline:** the
  cross-encoder was trained on SNLI+MNLI. WikiFactCheck-English pairs are
  Wikipedia-derived claim/counter-claim pairs, structurally close to MNLI's
  genre, and 167 of our 500 dissimilar positives come from SNLI's *validation*
  split, which shares its distribution with the model's training data. Its 0.993
  on phase 1 should therefore be read as an upper bound.
- **Direct evidence of artefact sensitivity in our scorers:** architecture 2's
  paraphrase false positives are concentrated in SemAntoNeg (the source-split
  table in §5), whose paraphrases are *not + antonym* constructions. That is an
  artefact of how that corpus was built, and it inflates architecture 2's
  apparent weakness on paraphrases relative to what a real student would write.
- **Not measurable here:** whether the cross-encoder would hold up on
  human-written student negations. Nothing in this experiment tests that; it
  needs human-written data.

### 6.5 Relation to experiment 1 (negation direction)

Experiment 1 found a negation axis that ranked generator-made negations above
their base in 100% of held-out explicit pairs, and found that the *direction* of
the embedding shift separated negation from paraphrase (AUC 0.95–0.99) where the
*distance* (raw cosine) could not (AUC 0.50). Experiment 2 tests the same claim
on data the generator did not make:

- **The direction-over-distance finding does not survive.** `delta_p` — the
  projection shift that carried experiment 1 — scores 0.5661 AUC on phase 1,
  0.5085 on phase 2 and 0.4433 on xNot360 with MiniLM, and 0.5504 / 0.5058 /
  0.4811 with mpnet. Raw cosine is no better (MiniLM 0.5231 / 0.7381 / 0.3146),
  so the ordering "direction beats distance" technically holds on phase 1, but
  at a level (0.57 vs 0.52) with no practical content.
- **The lexical baseline's collapse is the sharpest cross-experiment
  contrast.** The same `cue_count` function scored 1.00 pairwise accuracy on
  experiment 1's explicit negations and 0.492 AUC here. Experiment 1's lexicon
  was not measuring negation; it was measuring the generator, which builds
  negations out of that lexicon.
- **The cancellation failure reappears.** Experiment 1 found that MiniLM pushes
  K1 cancellations (*"never fails to sort"*) as far along the negation axis as a
  plain negation. Experiment 2's architecture 2 makes the mirror-image error on
  real cancellations (*"not an evil idea"* matched to *"not a good idea"*). Both
  experiments say the same thing: these representations count negative surface
  material rather than composing it.

## 7. Caveats and threats to validity

Ranked by how much each could change the conclusions.

1. **CANNOT's label noise and construction (could change the ranking).** 47% of
   phase-1 positives fall in the derived `other` bucket, and inspection (§2.5)
   shows many are factual substitutions rather than negations
   (*nephew → grandson*). At least one positive has A identical to B. A scorer
   that is good at "did the meaning change?" is rewarded, and one that is good
   at "was this negated?" is not fully credited. The cross-encoder is a
   meaning-change detector, so part of its margin may be this.
2. **The NLI cross-encoder's training distribution (could change the margin
   size, not its sign).** Trained on SNLI+MNLI; 167 of the 500 dissimilar
   positives are SNLI validation pairs and much of CANNOT is MNLI-like. Its
   phase-1 0.993 is an upper bound. Its xNot360 result (0.940 AUC on data it was
   not trained on) argues the sign of the conclusion survives.
3. **Threshold selection objective (changes every operating point).**
   Thresholds maximise F1 on balanced training data. For grading, the objective
   should be a bound on the paraphrase false-positive rate. At a strict margin
   threshold of 0 instead of the fitted −0.017, architecture 2's paraphrase FPR
   falls from 0.395 to 0.178 while its recall falls from 0.766 to 0.646 — so the
   headline FPRs are an artefact of the objective as much as of the method.
4. **Phase 2's class composition (inflates every AUC).** 500 of 1,001 negatives
   are unrelated pairs, which are easy. Phase-2 AUCs are therefore not
   comparable to phase-1 AUCs, and the phase-1 numbers are the ones to quote for
   the negation-vs-paraphrase question.
5. **Embedding-model dependence (measured, and large).** Swapping MiniLM for
   mpnet moves architecture 1's phase-2 AUC from 0.734 to 0.407 and the hybrid's
   from 0.777 to 0.507, while moving architecture 2's from 0.686 to 0.752 and
   its paraphrase false-positive rate from 0.395 to 0.192. Any claim about which
   of our architectures is better is conditional on the embedding model; only
   the comparison against the NLI baseline is robust to it. A third model was
   not run.
6. **Subsampling of the NLI similarity function (300 of 2,001–3,002 pairs,
   reference sets capped at 8).** Its AUCs carry sampling error of roughly ±0.03
   at n=300; the comparison against plain NLI is made on the same 300 pairs, so
   the *relative* claim is safe, the absolute one less so.
7. **Derived negation types.** verbal/antonym/other is a heuristic partition
   (§2.2); the per-type recall table inherits its errors.
8. **Leakage risk is low but not zero.** Sentence-level disjointness is enforced
   and measured at 0 overlap, but CANNOT contains near-duplicate sentences
   (Wikipedia claims re-used across pairs) that normalisation does not catch;
   two sentences differing by one token could sit on opposite sides of the
   split.
9. **Single random seed.** Seed 0 throughout — for the phase assignment, the
   fold assignment and the subsampling. No seed-sensitivity analysis was run.
10. **Generator coverage measured on this data only.** 3–4% empty NEG sets here;
    a different input distribution (real student answers, questions,
    imperatives) could be much worse.

## 8. Decisions made that were not in the brief

| decision | what was done | why |
|---|---|---|
| CANNOT source attribution | Recovered by exact text match against the per-source files in CANNOT's own repository | The brief requires "record the source of every pair" but the released TSV has no source column |
| Negation-type derivation | Source-first, then cue-count change, then WordNet antonym swap (§2.2) | The brief says "if derivable"; CANNOT has no such label |
| Disjointness strictness | Enforced on all sentences, not only on A | The brief says "no sentence may appear in both phase 1 and phase 2"; that is stronger than deduplicating on A, so the stronger rule was used |
| Quota overshoot | Phases contain 3,002 and 2,001 pairs instead of 3,000 and 2,000 | Pairs sharing an A are assigned as a group to avoid leakage; the last group can cross the quota |
| SAME set contents | A itself is always included in SAME | Otherwise a paraphrase has nothing to match when the generator produced only NEG variants |
| Empty-set sentinel | `s_neg = −1` (cosine) / `−2` (residual) when the NEG set is empty | Pushes coverage failures towards "not a negation" rather than leaving them undefined; the alternative (dropping the pair) would hide the failure |
| "Unrelated" threshold | 5th percentile of the training pairs' max-similarity | Phase 1 has no unrelated pairs to fit against |
| NLI subsampling | 300 pairs per split, reference sets capped at the 8 nearest by cosine | The full computation is ~110k cross-encoder passes; measured throughput is 13–16 pairs/s on this machine |
| Similarity (ii) definition | `resid(v)·resid(B) − \|p(v) − p(B)\|` | The brief says "c_resid combined with polarity agreement" without specifying the combination |
| Cosine baseline orientation | Score = `−c_raw` (lower similarity ⇒ more likely a negation) | The natural reading of "raw cosine alone" as a negation score; §4 shows this orientation is wrong on phase 2, which is itself reported rather than silently flipped |
| Embedding implementation | `transformers` mean-pooling rather than the `sentence-transformers` package | Identical pipeline for these two models; avoids a dependency, and reuses the first experiment's cache |
| Module name | The experiment module is `detect.py`, not `experiment.py` | Avoids a module-name collision with `experiments/negation_direction/experiment.py`, which is imported for `embed`/`cue_count`/`unit` |
| Extra architecture-3 feature | Both margins added, not one | They are cheap and the brief did not forbid it; their opposing coefficients are reported in §4 |
| mpnet run | Added after the fact, `--no-nli` | This report asks for a scorer × model grid; the NLI baseline does not depend on the embedding model |

## 10. Open questions and recommended next steps

**Things that were guessed and need a human decision**

1. **What counts as a negation for grading.** CANNOT says *nephew → grandson* is
   one. If the project disagrees, 47% of the positives used here are
   mislabelled for the purpose, and every number changes. This is the single
   most consequential open question.
2. **The operating point.** Everything here maximises F1. A grading system needs
   a maximum acceptable paraphrase false-positive rate, and that number has to
   come from the project, not from the data.
3. **What to do with "unrelated".** Half of phase 2's negatives are neither a
   negation nor a paraphrase, and a binary scorer must call them something. The
   three-way decision reaches 0.754 accuracy; whether that is the right output
   shape is a product decision.

**Next experiments, in priority order**

1. **Get human-written negation pairs.** Every conclusion here is bounded by
   CANNOT being machine-made. A few hundred teacher/student pairs written by
   people would be worth more than any further modelling on this data.
2. **Fine-tune a small cross-encoder on generator output and test it on human
   data.** The generator's real value looks like training-data production, not
   runtime oracle: it produced 70,451 labelled variants for 3,924 sentences in
   about three minutes.
3. **Calibrate for the paraphrase-FPR objective** and re-report every scorer at
   a fixed FPR (say 2%) rather than at max-F1. That is the number a grading
   system lives or dies by, and it is cheap to produce from the existing score
   files.
4. **Test the cross-encoder against the HSS paper's fixed 0.7 penalty
   directly.** Nothing in this experiment compares against the mechanism the
   project is trying to replace; a direct comparison on the same pairs is the
   obvious missing baseline.
5. **Fix architecture 2's set asymmetry** if it is worth pursuing: balance the
   SAME set with generated paraphrases, or normalise the margin by set size.
   The current 16-vs-2 imbalance is a known, quantified defect (§4, §5).
6. **Run a third embedding model and more seeds.** Two models already disagree
   about which architecture is best (§6.1); two points do not establish a
   pattern.
